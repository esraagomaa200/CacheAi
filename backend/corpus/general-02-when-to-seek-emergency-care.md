# When to Seek Emergency Care

Timely access to emergency care saves lives. The World Health Organization has stated that putting in place proficient emergency care could address nearly half of all deaths and over one third of disabilities in low- and middle-income countries — making prehospital and facility-based emergency care one of the highest-impact, most cost-effective forms of secondary prevention available in any health system. In 2019, WHO Member States unanimously adopted World Health Assembly resolution 72.16, committing to build emergency and trauma care systems as part of universal health coverage, precisely because getting the right care fast, for the right condition, changes outcomes dramatically.

## The Core Idea: When in Doubt, Seek Care

Many people hesitate to call for emergency help, worried about "overreacting" or wasting resources. But for the conditions below, waiting to see if symptoms improve on their own can cost precious time that cannot be recovered later. If any of these warning signs are present, treat it as an emergency and seek help immediately rather than waiting.

## General Emergency Warning Signs

These "red flag" signs — recognized across major health authorities including the CDC and the American Heart Association — call for an immediate emergency response, regardless of the underlying cause:

- **Chest pain or pressure**, especially if it spreads to the arm, jaw, back, or shoulder, or is accompanied by sweating, nausea, or shortness of breath — a possible heart attack.
- **Sudden stroke-like signs** — face drooping, arm weakness, or slurred speech (the FAST test) — even if the symptoms pass quickly.
- **Sudden or severe difficulty breathing** — gasping, choking, unable to speak full sentences, or lips/skin turning pale, blue, or grey.
- **Loss of consciousness, fainting, or a seizure**, especially if it is the first time or the person does not quickly return to normal.
- **Heavy or uncontrolled bleeding** that does not stop with firm, direct pressure.
- **Sudden, severe confusion** or a marked change in mental state that is out of character.
- **A sudden, severe headache** unlike any experienced before, especially with vision changes, vomiting, or neck stiffness.
- **Signs of a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis)** — swelling of the lips, tongue, or throat, hives spreading rapidly, difficulty breathing, or dizziness/collapse after a known exposure (food, medication, insect sting).

## In Children and Infants

Emergency signs in babies and young children can look different from adults and should not be ignored — this includes difficulty breathing, poor feeding, unusual sleepiness or difficulty waking, a bluish tint to the skin or lips, or noticeably fewer wet diapers than normal (a sign of dehydration).

## What To Do

If you recognize any of these signs in yourself or someone near you, call emergency services immediately — in Egypt, dial **123** for an ambulance, or **112** for the unified emergency line. Give your exact location, describe what is happening, and follow any first-aid instructions the dispatcher gives you while help is on the way. Do not attempt to drive a seriously unwell person to the hospital yourself if calling an ambulance is possible — professional responders can begin assessment and treatment before arrival, which often makes a real difference to the outcome.

## The Bottom Line

Emergency warning signs exist precisely because certain conditions cannot safely wait. Acting quickly, even when uncertain, gives the best chance of a good outcome — and health systems, including Egypt's, are built and staffed specifically to respond to these calls.
