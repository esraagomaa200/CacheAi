# Asthma Attacks — Recognizing and Responding

An asthma attack is when asthma symptoms become severe and it becomes difficult to breathe. It can be life-threatening, so it needs to be taken seriously and treated right away — it is not something to "wait out."

## Recognizing an Asthma Attack

Signs that someone is having an asthma attack include worsening wheezing, coughing, and chest tightness, increasing shortness of breath or gasping for breath, difficulty speaking in full sentences (speaking only in short bursts), a rapid pulse, and flushed, pale, or bluish-looking skin, especially around the lips.

## What to Do During an Asthma Attack

1. **Sit up straight.** This helps breathing. Do not lie down. Try to stay calm, as panic can make breathing harder.
2. **Use the reliever inhaler.** If it is a standard blue reliever inhaler, take one puff every 30 to 60 seconds, up to a maximum of 10 puffs. If it is a combination AIR or MART-type inhaler, take one puff every 1 to 3 minutes, up to a maximum of 6 puffs. Shake the inhaler between puffs, and use a spacer device if one is available, as this helps more medicine reach the lungs.
3. **Follow a personal asthma action plan** if the person has one — it may give slightly different specific instructions tailored to their condition.
4. **Do not leave the person alone** during the attack. If you need to get help, send someone else while you stay with them, or keep your phone with you.

## When to Call Emergency Services

Call emergency services (999 in the UK, 123 in Egypt) if any of the following happen:

- The person starts to feel worse at any point during the attack
- They do not feel better after taking the maximum dose from their reliever inhaler
- They do not have an asthma inhaler with them
- Breathing becomes severe, with significant sweating, faintness, nausea, panting, a rapid pulse, or pale, cold, and moist skin

If symptoms have not improved after about 10 minutes and the ambulance has not yet arrived, the reliever inhaler can be used again at the same maximum dose while continuing to wait for help. Never attempt to drive the person to the emergency department yourself — call an ambulance so treatment can start as soon as possible and so paramedics can monitor the person during transport.

## After an Attack

Even after symptoms settle, a severe asthma attack, or one that required emergency treatment, should always be followed up with a doctor. It is often a sign that the person's regular asthma management plan needs to be reviewed.

## Reducing Future Risk

Knowing personal asthma warning signs, avoiding known triggers, taking preventer medication consistently as prescribed (not just the reliever inhaler), and following medical advice all help reduce the frequency and severity of future asthma attacks.
