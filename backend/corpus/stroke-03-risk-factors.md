# Risk Factors for Stroke

High blood pressure, high cholesterol, smoking, obesity, and diabetes are among the leading causes of stroke. According to the CDC, about one in three US adults has at least one of these conditions or risk factors, which means a very large share of stroke risk is potentially preventable or manageable.

## Health Conditions That Raise Stroke Risk

- **High blood pressure** is the single leading cause of stroke. It puts extra strain on blood vessel walls throughout the body, including in the brain, over time making them more likely to narrow, rupture, or become blocked by a clot. High blood pressure is also the main reason people with diabetes have an increased risk of stroke.
- **High cholesterol** contributes to the buildup of fatty deposits in arteries (atherosclerosis), which can narrow the blood vessels supplying the brain or break off and travel there as a clot.
- **Heart disease**, including atrial fibrillation (an irregular heartbeat), coronary artery disease, and heart valve defects, can all increase the chance that a clot forms and travels to the brain.
- **Diabetes** causes excess sugar to build up in the blood, which can damage blood vessels over time and prevent oxygen and nutrients from reaching parts of the body, including the brain — significantly raising stroke risk.
- **Sickle cell disease** and other conditions that affect blood cells and clotting can also increase stroke risk, particularly in younger patients.

## Behaviors That Raise Stroke Risk

- **Smoking** damages the heart and blood vessels. Nicotine raises blood pressure, and the carbon monoxide in cigarette smoke reduces the amount of oxygen the blood can carry, both of which increase stroke risk. Quitting smoking at any age lowers stroke risk over time.
- **Unhealthy diet**, particularly one high in saturated fat, trans fat, and sodium, contributes to the conditions above.
- **Physical inactivity** is linked to obesity, high blood pressure, and high cholesterol, all of which raise stroke risk.
- **Heavy or regular alcohol use** can raise blood pressure and contribute to stroke risk.

## Factors You Cannot Change

- **Age:** Stroke risk increases with age, but a stroke can happen to anyone at any age, including children.
- **Family history and genetics:** A family history of stroke raises personal risk.
- **Sex:** Stroke risk and outcomes differ somewhat between men and women, and stroke remains a leading cause of death and long-term disability among women.
- **Race and ethnicity:** Some racial and ethnic groups face substantially higher stroke risk than others, reflecting a combination of biological and social/environmental factors.
- **Personal history:** A previous stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA/"mini-stroke") significantly raises the risk of a future stroke.

## Lowering Your Risk

Many stroke risk factors can be managed. Steps that help include: getting blood pressure checked regularly and treated if high, managing cholesterol and diabetes with a healthcare provider, quitting smoking, eating a diet lower in sodium and saturated fat, staying physically active, limiting alcohol, and taking any prescribed heart or blood-thinning medication exactly as directed. If you already have heart disease, high cholesterol, high blood pressure, or diabetes, working with a doctor to control these conditions is one of the most effective ways to lower stroke risk.
