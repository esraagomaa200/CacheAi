# Choking First Aid for Adults

Choking happens when an object, such as food, blocks someone's airway, either partly or completely. It can quickly become life-threatening because it stops or severely reduces the amount of oxygen reaching the lungs and brain, so it needs an immediate response.

## Recognizing Choking

A choking person often cannot speak, cry out, cough forcefully, or breathe properly. They may clutch at their throat, their face may turn red then bluish, and they may look panicked. If they can still cough forcefully and are able to make sounds, the airway is only partly blocked — encourage them to keep coughing, as this is often the most effective way to clear it on its own. Stay with them and monitor closely in case it worsens.

## Severe Choking — Step by Step

If the person cannot cough, speak, or breathe (severe or complete blockage), act immediately:

1. **Up to 5 back blows.** Stand slightly behind and to the side of the person. Support their chest with one hand and lean them forward so that if the object is dislodged, it comes out of the mouth rather than going further down. Using the heel of your other hand, give up to 5 sharp, firm blows between their shoulder blades, checking after each one to see if the blockage has cleared.
2. **Up to 5 abdominal thrusts, if back blows don't work.** Stand behind the person. Link your hands together between their belly button and the bottom of their chest, with your lower hand clenched in a fist. Pull sharply inwards and upwards, up to 5 times, checking after each thrust.
3. **Call emergency services** if the object still has not come out after this. Continue alternating cycles of 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts until the blockage clears, help arrives, or the person becomes unresponsive.

## Important Exceptions

Do not give abdominal thrusts to babies under 1 year old or to pregnant women — different techniques (such as chest thrusts) are used for infants, and pressure should be applied higher on the chest, not the abdomen, for pregnant women. If you have not been trained in infant choking first aid, follow the guidance of the emergency call operator.

## If the Person Becomes Unresponsive

If the person stops responding, carefully support them to the ground and check their breathing. If they are not breathing, or not breathing normally, begin CPR immediately — chest compressions can sometimes help dislodge the object as well as maintaining blood flow — and continue with the guidance of the emergency services operator on the phone until an ambulance arrives.

## After a Choking Episode

Even if the blockage clears and the person seems fine afterward, they should be seen by a doctor if abdominal thrusts were used, since these can occasionally cause internal injury, or if there is any ongoing throat discomfort, cough, or difficulty swallowing following the episode.
