# Warning Signs of a Heart Attack

A heart attack happens when blood flow to part of the heart is blocked, usually by a clot, starving the heart muscle of oxygen. Recognizing the warning signs quickly and calling for emergency help right away can save heart muscle and save a life — the American Heart Association is clear: don't wait to get help if you have any heart attack warning signs, and call emergency services immediately, even if you're uncertain whether it's really a heart attack.

## Chest Discomfort

The most common warning sign in both men and women is chest discomfort. Most people who have a heart attack feel discomfort in the center of the chest that lasts more than a few minutes, or that goes away and comes back. It doesn't always feel like sharp pain — it is often described as uncomfortable pressure, squeezing, fullness, or an ache in the chest.

## Discomfort in Other Areas of the Upper Body

Heart attack symptoms are not limited to the chest. Discomfort or pain can also occur in one or both arms, the back, neck, jaw, or stomach.

## Shortness of Breath

Shortness of breath can occur with or without chest discomfort, and can be a warning sign on its own. Any sudden or unexplained difficulty breathing, especially combined with fatigue or discomfort, should be taken seriously.

## Other Possible Signs

Additional symptoms that can accompany a heart attack include breaking out in a cold sweat, nausea, lightheadedness, or a feeling similar to indigestion.

## Symptoms Can Differ Between Men and Women

While chest pain or discomfort remains the most common symptom for both men and women, women are somewhat more likely than men to experience the other, less "classic" symptoms — particularly shortness of breath, nausea or vomiting, and back or jaw pain — sometimes without prominent chest pain at all. This can make heart attacks in women harder to recognize, which is why any combination of these symptoms should be treated seriously regardless of how "typical" it looks.

## What to Do

If you or someone near you experiences any of these warning signs:

1. Call emergency services immediately — do not drive yourself or wait to see if symptoms pass.
2. Stay as calm and still as possible while waiting for help; sitting down and resting reduces strain on the heart.
3. If instructed by a medical dispatcher or if not allergic, chewing an aspirin can help while waiting for the ambulance.
4. If the person becomes unresponsive and is not breathing normally, be ready to begin CPR immediately and continue until help arrives.

Because heart muscle begins to die within minutes of blood flow being cut off, faster treatment means less permanent heart damage. Acting immediately — even if the symptoms are mild, intermittent, or "don't feel like a real emergency" — gives the best chance of a full recovery.
