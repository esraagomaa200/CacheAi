# Angina vs Heart Attack — Knowing the Difference

Angina is a feeling of pain or tightness, usually in the chest, caused by reduced blood flow to the heart muscle — it can be a sign of an underlying heart problem, so it's important to see a doctor as soon as possible if you think you may have it. Understanding how angina normally behaves helps you recognize when chest symptoms have crossed the line into a possible heart attack, which needs an emergency response.

## What Angina Feels Like

The pain or discomfort of angina may feel like tightness, squeezing, or pressure, or like a dull ache, usually in the chest but sometimes spreading to the arms, neck, jaw, or back. It can be accompanied by nausea, breathlessness, dizziness, or sweating. Angina episodes are typically triggered by physical exertion, emotional stress, cold weather, or a heavy meal — situations that increase the heart's demand for oxygen beyond what narrowed arteries can supply.

## How Long It Lasts and What Relieves It

A defining feature of stable angina is that it usually lasts less than 10 minutes and should ease after resting for a few minutes or taking prescribed angina medicine (such as a GTN spray). If the trigger stops and the pain fades with rest or medication within that window, it is more likely to be a typical angina episode rather than a heart attack.

## When Angina Becomes a Medical Emergency

Call emergency services (999 in the UK, 123 in Egypt) immediately if you or someone else has:

- Chest pain or discomfort that does not go away after resting or after taking angina medicine (including a second dose)
- Sudden pain or discomfort in the chest that persists and does not ease
- Pain spreading to the arms, neck, jaw, shoulders, stomach, or back
- Chest pain together with sweating, feeling sick, feeling light-headed, or shortness of breath

Any of these combinations can indicate a heart attack rather than ordinary angina, and requires urgent hospital treatment — do not wait to see if it passes.

## When to Seek Urgent (But Not Emergency) Care

Contact a doctor or urgent care line the same day, rather than waiting for a routine appointment, if angina symptoms are changing in a concerning way — for example, if known angina symptoms:

- Come and go in a pattern that feels different from usual
- Feel worse than before
- Are happening more often or lasting longer than before
- Are happening even when resting, rather than only during exertion

This pattern can indicate "unstable angina," which carries a higher short-term risk of progressing to a heart attack and needs medical assessment promptly, even if it doesn't meet the criteria above for calling an ambulance right now.

## The Key Distinction

In short: predictable chest discomfort that is clearly tied to exertion, lasts only a few minutes, and resolves with rest or medication is more typical of stable angina. Chest discomfort that is new, severe, prolonged, unrelieved by rest or medication, or accompanied by sweating, nausea, breathlessness, or spreading pain should always be treated as a possible heart attack and responded to as a medical emergency.
