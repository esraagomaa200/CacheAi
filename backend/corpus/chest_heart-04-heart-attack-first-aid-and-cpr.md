# Heart Attack First Aid and Hands-Only CPR

If you suspect someone is having a heart attack, the single most important action is to call emergency services immediately. What you do in the minutes before the ambulance arrives can make a real difference to the outcome.

## First Aid While Waiting for the Ambulance (NHS guidance)

- **Sit and rest.** Help the person sit and rest on the floor in a comfortable position, with their knees bent and their back supported — this reduces strain on the heart while waiting.
- **Aspirin, if available.** If the person is not allergic to it, they can chew a 300mg aspirin tablet while waiting for the ambulance, as this may help reduce clotting. Do not give aspirin if there is any known allergy or if a dispatcher advises against it.
- **GTN spray, if prescribed.** If the person has diagnosed angina and has been prescribed a GTN (glyceryl trinitrate) spray, they can use it as directed.
- **Stay with them.** Keep monitoring their responsiveness and breathing. Stay calm and keep the person calm — anxiety increases heart strain.
- **Do not drive them yourself.** Paramedics can begin treatment in the ambulance and hospitals can prepare in advance once notified, which is not possible if you drive the person yourself.

## If the Person Becomes Unresponsive and Stops Breathing Normally

If the person loses consciousness and is not breathing, or is only gasping, this is cardiac arrest and CPR must start immediately — every minute without CPR or defibrillation drastically reduces the chance of survival.

## Hands-Only CPR (American Heart Association)

For an adult who has suddenly collapsed, the American Heart Association recommends Hands-Only CPR, which consists of just two steps and does not require rescue breaths — this makes it far more likely that an untrained bystander will actually step in and help:

1. **Call for emergency help first** (or have someone else call) so professional responders and, if available, an automated external defibrillator (AED) are on the way.
2. **Push hard and fast in the center of the chest.** Kneel beside the person. Place the heel of one hand on the center of the chest, and the heel of your other hand on top, lacing your fingers together. Position your shoulders directly over your hands and keep your arms straight.

Compressions should be:

- **At least 2 inches (about 5 cm) deep**
- **At a rate of 100 to 120 compressions per minute** — roughly the tempo of the songs "Stayin' Alive" or "Uptown Funk," which can help maintain the right pace.

Continue chest compressions without interruption until emergency help arrives, an AED becomes available and ready to use, or the person begins to breathe normally on their own. Do not stop to check for a pulse repeatedly — sustained, uninterrupted compressions give the best chance of keeping oxygenated blood moving to the brain and vital organs until professional help takes over.

## Key Takeaway

Whether the person is still conscious with chest pain, or has collapsed and stopped breathing normally, the first action is always the same: call for emergency help immediately, and do not wait to see if things improve on their own.
