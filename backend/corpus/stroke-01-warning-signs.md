# Stroke Warning Signs — The FAST Test

A stroke is a medical emergency. The main symptoms can be remembered with the word **FAST**:

- **F — Face:** One side of the face may droop (fall) and it might be hard to smile. The mouth or eye may have dropped.
- **A — Arms:** The person may not be able to fully lift both arms and keep them there, because of weakness or numbness in one arm.
- **S — Speech:** Speech may be slurred or garbled, or the person may not be able to talk at all despite appearing to be awake. They may also have trouble understanding what you are saying.
- **T — Time:** It's time to call emergency services immediately if you see any of these signs.

## Other Symptoms of Stroke

Stroke can cause a wide range of symptoms depending on the part of the brain affected. In addition to the FAST signs, a stroke can also cause:

- Weakness or numbness down one side of the body
- Blurred vision or loss of sight in one or both eyes
- Difficulty finding words or speaking, even without slurring
- Confusion and memory loss
- Feeling dizzy or suddenly falling over, loss of balance or coordination
- A sudden, severe headache unlike any before
- Feeling or being sick (nausea or vomiting)

## What to Do Immediately

If you notice any single one of these symptoms in yourself or someone else, treat it as an emergency and call for emergency medical help right away. Every minute without treatment, brain cells are dying — the faster a person having a stroke gets treated, the less damage is likely to happen.

An important warning: symptoms of a stroke can sometimes stop after a short time, making the person feel like they are okay again. This does **not** mean the danger has passed — it may be a transient ischaemic attack (TIA), sometimes called a "mini-stroke," which is itself a serious warning sign of a full stroke to come. Even if symptoms go away completely, get medical help immediately. This guidance also applies if the symptoms appeared and resolved at any point within the last 24 hours.

## While Waiting for Help

- Do not let the person eat or drink anything, since swallowing may be affected.
- Do not give them aspirin (unlike a suspected heart attack) — some strokes are caused by bleeding in the brain, and aspirin can make this worse.
- Keep the person safe, calm, and lying down with the head slightly raised if possible, and monitor their breathing until help arrives.
- Do not attempt to drive the person to hospital yourself; call for an ambulance so that treatment can begin as soon as possible, including in transit.

Recognizing the FAST signs and acting on them immediately is the single most important thing a bystander can do to improve a stroke patient's chances of recovery.
